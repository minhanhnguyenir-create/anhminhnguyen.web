Reflection on Digital Humanities

Date: October 27, 2026 | Author: Anh Minh Nguyen

In the lecture "Digital Humanities (DH) Introduction: From the Internet to Interactive Scholarship" by Orla Murphy, I learned how the internet and markup languages changed the way we study humanities. This topic is very interesting to me because it shows how technology helps us understand human knowledge better.

First, the lecture explains the beginning of the internet. In 1969, a network called ARPANET connected four universities. The speaker mentions a funny story: they tried to send the word "LOGIN", but the system crashed after just two letters: "LO". This small failure shows that early technology was very weak, but it was the first step for global academic collaboration.

However, the biggest change came from Tim Berners-Lee in 1989. He created the World Wide Web not just for data, but for "human-readable information". I strongly agree with Berners-Lee's idea that information gets meaning through relationships. In the humanities, we always look for connections between texts, authors, and history. The Web's three pillars (URLs, HTTP, and HTML) made these connections possible.

"HTML separates content from presentation. The # tag means 'this is a major heading'—whether it appears large and bold is a separate decision."

This quote from the lecture shows why HTML is so important. It taught scholars that structure matters. By using tags, we give semantic meaning to our writing. Computers can understand the purpose of the text, not just the appearance.

Furthermore, I want to highlight the Perseus Digital Library project (1992) mentioned in the slides. Gregory Crane created a library where readers can click on a Greek word to see its grammar and translations. This is not just simple digitization; it is augmented reading. It changes the way we read and study ancient texts.

In conclusion, as the lecture notes, markup languages did not create modern AI. But they made human knowledge easier to organize and learn from. The transition from human reading to machine processing is built on these early foundations. This lecture helped me understand that digital humanities is a bridge between traditional texts and modern technology.
